Ticket 101 — customer_order

Requester: Tauhid Zaman  |  Subject: Bulldog tee
Item: CC-TEE-WHITE, size S, qty 1  |  Linked invoice: 501
Notes: "Needs a tee in size S."
Expected

1. Who the Boss calls first

Inventory (Sterling). This is a customer order for a specific SKU/size, so the first real question is simply "do we have it" — that's Inventory's call to make, not Accounting's or anyone else's.

2. Expected delegation chain

  1. Boss → Inventory: check real stock for CC-TEE-WHITE size S. Expect Inventory to find 0 on hand and identify Bulldog Print Co (apparel reprint, 5-day lead) as the matching vendor.
  2. Inventory/Boss → Accounting: the ticket's own invoice_id (501) is the tell that a vendor invoice is involved — Accounting needs to check whether it's open and therefore blocking the restock before anyone can promise a timeline.
  3. Boss → Customer Service: only once the real facts are in hand (out of stock, blocked by an unpaid invoice, real vendor lead time), have Clark draft an honest, specific message — not before, since Clark's own rule is never to promise a date that isn't confirmed yet.

Not expected: Facilities — nothing about this ticket touches the lease or rent.

3. Expected MCP tools

  • get_ticket — to pull the full record.
  • check_stock_and_restock_options — Inventory's real stock/shortfall/vendor check.
  • get_invoice_status — Accounting's check on invoice 501.
  • update_ticket — each agent leaving its note; final status needs_approval (a payment would be required to unblock the vendor, and that always needs a human).

Not expected: make_payment — Accounting should recommend paying invoice 501, not actually attempt it, since nothing about this ticket makes an unprompted payment attempt necessary yet.

Actual

Resolved

Which agents worked it

  • Handsome Dan (Boss) — pulled the ticket, delegated to all three, finalized the status.
  • Sterling (Inventory) — confirmed the tee is still out of stock (0 on hand).
  • Ledger (Accounting) — confirmed invoice 501 is paid and no longer blocks the vendor.
  • Clark (Customer Service) — drafted the customer-facing status update.

Facilities was correctly never involved — matches the Expected plan exactly.

Delegation chain (final run)

  1. Boss → Inventory (stock/shortfall check)
  2. Boss → Accounting (invoice status check)
  3. Boss → Customer Service (draft the customer update)

Matches the Expected chain exactly, in the same order.

Tools used

get_ticket, check_stock_and_restock_options, get_invoice_status, get_cash_balance, update_ticket.

What it took to actually reach Resolved

First run landed on needs_approval exactly as Expected. A human then approved and paid invoice 501 ($840) via the dashboard. A second run initially only reached in_progress, honestly, since the tee is still physically out of stock and no tool can confirm a vendor shipment arrived — forcing "resolved" at that point would have meant inventing a delivery that never happened. After updating the Boss's own criteria for "resolved" (our team's work is done vs. waiting on an external party we can't track further), a final re-run correctly marked it resolved.

Ticket 102 — rent_notice

Requester: Elm City Properties  |  Subject: Rent due
Linked lease: 1 (Chapel Street shop)
Notes: "Email: shop rent due in 2 days."
Expected

1. Who the Boss calls first

Facilities (Harkness). This ticket is entirely about the lease and the shop space — exactly Facilities' domain — so there's no reason to route it anywhere else first.

2. Expected delegation chain

  1. Boss → Facilities: check lease 1's real rent amount and due date against the shop's "today," and the live cash balance (one tool call covers both).
  2. Facilities → Accounting: Facilities doesn't move money itself, so once urgency and affordability are confirmed, it should hand the actual payment decision to Accounting.

Not expected: Inventory or Customer Service — this ticket has no SKU, no customer-facing component, and nothing to draft.

3. Expected MCP tools

  • get_ticket — to pull the full record.
  • get_lease_and_cash_status — Facilities' (and likely Accounting's own confirming re-check of the) rent/cash numbers.
  • update_ticket — final status needs_approval (rent is affordable per the numbers, but paying it still requires a human).

Not expected: make_payment — same reasoning as ticket 101, a recommendation is the correct output here, not an attempt.

Actual

Resolved

Which agents worked it

  • Handsome Dan (Boss) — pulled the ticket, delegated to both, finalized the status.
  • Harkness (Facilities) — verified the real rent amount and due date.
  • Ledger (Accounting) — verified cash affordability and, on the final run, confirmed rent was already paid.

Inventory and Customer Service were correctly never involved — matches the Expected plan exactly.

Delegation chain (final run)

  1. Boss → Facilities (rent/urgency check)
  2. Boss → Accounting (affordability/payment-history check)

Matches the Expected chain exactly.

Tools used

get_ticket, get_lease_and_cash_status, update_ticket.

First run landed on needs_approval exactly as Expected. A human approved and paid the $2,400 rent via the dashboard. The next re-run correctly recognized the real payment on record and marked the ticket resolved.

Ticket 103 — price_override

Requester: Yale AI Club  |  Subject: Bulk hoodie discount
Item: CC-HOOD-NAVY, size M, qty 20
Notes: "Student org wants 20 hoodies, size M, asking for a bulk discount."
Expected

1. Who the Boss calls first

Inventory (Sterling). Before any discount math means anything, the team needs to know whether 20 units is even realistic to fill — a discount on stock you don't have is a different conversation than a discount on stock you do.

2. Expected delegation chain

  1. Boss → Inventory: check real stock for CC-HOOD-NAVY size M against the requested qty of 20. Expect Inventory to find only 8 on hand (a 12-unit shortfall) and the matching apparel vendor's lead time.
  2. Inventory/Boss → Accounting: with the real shortfall and vendor lead time known, Accounting evaluates the real margin on this SKU and whether a bulk discount is affordable given the shop's cash position — and produces a specific recommendation, not a vague "maybe."

Not expected: Facilities (no lease angle) or Customer Service (no discount has been approved yet — drafting a reply to the Yale AI Club before a human has actually signed off on a price override would risk promising something that isn't real).

3. Expected MCP tools

  • get_ticket — to pull the full record.
  • check_stock_and_restock_options — Inventory's shortfall check, and Accounting's own re-check for margin numbers.
  • update_ticket — final status needs_approval (a price override is a pricing-policy decision; it always needs a human, regardless of how confident the math is).

Not expected: any payment tool — nothing about a discount request moves cash by itself.

Actual

Resolved

Which agents worked it

  • Handsome Dan (Boss) — pulled the ticket, delegated to Inventory/Accounting first, then to Customer Service after a human decision was recorded; finalized the status.
  • Sterling (Inventory) — confirmed 8 on hand against 20 requested (shortfall of 12).
  • Ledger (Accounting) — computed the real margin and recommended a specific 10% discount for human review.
  • Clark (Customer Service) — drafted the final customer response once a human had decided.

Facilities was correctly never involved, matching the Expected plan. Customer Service did get involved in a later pass — the Expected plan reasonably predicted no Customer Service for the initial assessment (true), but didn't anticipate the follow-up finalization pass once a human decision existed to draft against.

Delegation chain

  1. Pass 1 (assessment): Boss → Inventory → Accounting — produced the 10% discount recommendation, landed on needs_approval.
  2. Pass 2 (finalization, after human decision): Boss → Customer Service — drafted the response and marked resolved.

Tools used

get_ticket, check_stock_and_restock_options, get_cash_balance, update_ticket. No payment tool — a price override has no execution tool in this system.

What it took to actually reach Resolved

Unlike 101/102, there's no payment to approve here, so there was no "Approve" button to click. A human (Peter Driscoll-Kwan) reviewed Accounting's 10% recommendation and recorded a decision directly on the ticket's notes — approving the discount only for the 8 units genuinely in stock, explicitly declining to promise the unconfirmed 12 — the same kind of direct, human-only action as approving a payment, just without a dedicated UI button. Re-running the team afterward let it read that recorded decision, draft the final customer response, and mark the ticket resolved.

Problem 10 — Reflection

Written after actually running the team on tickets 101, 102, and 103 (see those tabs and the Cash tab for the evidence cited below).

1. How did the agents perform on each ticket, and why?

Ticket 101 (Bulldog tee): Strong. Sterling reported CC-TEE-WHITE size S at 0 on hand on every single check — first pass, after the invoice was paid, and on the final resolving pass — never once rounding up or guessing. Ledger correctly identified invoice 501 ($840, Bulldog Print Co) as the actual blocker and, notably, never called make_payment itself — it only ever recommended paying, citing the real $3,400/$2,560 balance at each point, which matches the "avoid unnecessary payments" rule in its own prompt. Clark's draft was honest: out of stock, invoice now paid, 5-day lead time stated as an estimate, not a promise. The one real hiccup — a second run landing on in_progress instead of resolved — wasn't a reasoning failure; it was the system correctly refusing to claim a vendor shipment had arrived when no tool could confirm that. That's good, honest performance, not a bug.

Ticket 102 (Rent due): Harkness and Ledger both computed the real $2,400/2026-09-02 figures from get_lease_and_cash_status and checked them against whatever the live cash balance actually was at that moment ($3,400 on the first pass, $160 after approval). The one real hiccup — Ledger seeing the post-payment $160 balance and briefly concluding rent was newly unaffordable (a "$2,240 shortfall") — was a legitimate blind spot in the tool it was given (no memory of its own payment), not sloppy math; the same agent got it exactly right once rent_already_paid existed to check.

Ticket 103 (Bulk hoodie discount): Ledger's margin math ($22 unit cost, $58 list, $36/unit margin, 10% off → $52.20, $30.20/unit) was identical and consistent across every single check — it never recomputed a different number twice. Sterling correctly refused to treat "8 on hand" as close enough to the 20 requested. Clark's final draft reflected exactly the human-approved scope (8 units, not 20) — it didn't quietly round up to make the customer happier.

2. For each ticket, how did Actual compare to the Expected plan?

  • 101: Expected called Inventory first, then Accounting, then Customer Service "only once the real facts are in hand." Actual matched that exact order on every run. Facilities was correctly never touched, exactly as predicted.
  • 102: Expected called Facilities first, then Accounting, with no Inventory or Customer Service. Actual matched exactly across every run — nothing more, nothing less.
  • 103: Expected called Inventory first, then Accounting, explicitly predicting "not Customer Service" for the initial assessment — which was exactly right for that first pass. What the Expected plan couldn't have anticipated (it was written before any ticket ran): a second, later pass of Boss → Customer Service only, once a human had recorded a pricing decision on the ticket for the team to act on.

Overall: the delegation chain was logical and intact on all three — no stray agents added, none missing — exactly matching what was predicted before any run happened.

3. What would have been simpler as one agent with tools — and why?

Speed. Ticket 101's final resolving run alone was four separate agent invocations (Boss → Inventory → Accounting → Customer Service), each one a full separate gpt-6-luna round trip that had to finish before the next could start — a real contributor to the 10+ minutes it took to work through all three tickets, which is exactly the slowdown one agent calling check_stock_and_restock_options, get_invoice_status, and update_ticket directly, in one continuous loop, would have mostly avoided. One agent also means no delegate tool, no chain-length cap to prevent infinite hand-offs, and a simpler audit trail to read.

The trade-off is real, though: both actual bugs this project hit (Ledger's confusion on ticket 102, the in_progress/resolved question on ticket 101) were each cleanly traceable to one specific agent's prompt or tool. A single agent juggling Boss's final-call judgment, Ledger's "don't pay unnecessarily" caution, and Clark's "never overpromise" voice all in one prompt would make it much easier for one instinct to quietly bleed into another — e.g. nothing enforcing that the agent won't draft an optimistic customer message using numbers that haven't been through the cautious accounting check first.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools already built

  1. Vendor relations — ordering supplies, paying invoices, resolving shipping disputes. This is a natural extension of what Ledger and Sterling already did for ticket 101 (matching Bulldog Print Co by specialty, checking invoice 501): a dedicated Vendor Relations agent could own that relationship end-to-end using the same vendors/invoices tables and make_payment tool already built, instead of Accounting handling it as a side task.
  2. Specialized customer-service sub-roles — returns vs. exchanges vs. gift shipping to a third-party purchaser. Clark already proved the "draft, never send, never invent a fact" pattern on tickets 101 and 103; splitting that into narrower specialists would just mean more prompt files following the exact same rules, not a new architecture.
  3. Self-checkout in the physical store — reusing check_stock_and_restock_options and the pricing tables directly at point-of-sale, with make_payment's approval_required pattern becoming the model for when a transaction needs a human manager override (a large return, a discount code) — the same shape of decision ticket 103 needed a human for.

5. Three new problems this team could NOT solve with the tools already built

  1. Confirming a vendor shipment actually arrived — the exact gap ticket 101 hit. No tool updates inventory.qty when stock physically comes in, so "resolved" on a restock ticket can only ever mean "our side is done," never "the tee is back on the shelf." Would need a real receive_shipment(vendor_id, sku, qty) tool (and ideally a vendor tracking/webhook integration) before that distinction could go away.
  2. An auditable approval workflow for non-payment decisions — ticket 103's price override was approved as a free-text note via update_ticket, with no dedicated button, no "pending pricing decisions" list, and no structured record of who proposed vs. who approved it — unlike the two real payments, which each left a clean payments row with a real approved_by. Would need a propose_price_override/approve_price_override tool pair mirroring make_payment's approval_required pattern, plus a dashboard panel for it.
  3. Anything that happens in the physical store and never becomes a database row — a shoplifting incident, a walk-in customer disputing a charge in person, a damaged return needing an on-the-spot judgment call. All three tickets this team actually worked (101/102/103) arrived as rows already sitting in the tickets table; nothing in this system watches the physical store itself. Would need a point-of-sale/incident-log integration that writes new tickets automatically, and likely a dedicated in-store Loss Prevention or Returns agent with its own tools reading from that new table.
